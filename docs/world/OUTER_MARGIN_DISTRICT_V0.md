# Outer Margin — Dangerous, Living, Excluded (District Profile v0.1)

> **Status:** Draft fiction and spatial planning. Player living **in** this district and the distinction between marginality and ruin are confirmed user design directions. Place names, neighborhoods, factions, institutions and specific encounters below remain proposals. Companion: [City Foundation](CITY_FOUNDATION_V0.md), [City Layout](CITY_LAYOUT_V0.md), [Top-down Map](CITY_PLAN_V0.svg).
>
> This is **not** a ruined wasteland, universal criminal slum, lawless apocalypse, generic neon dystopia or hopeless impoverishment simulator. Poverty is real and severe; the residents are not necessarily destitute, criminal or miserable.

## Character: the city behind its official face

**Working in-world administrative label:** Outer Margin. **Locals' nickname:** The Margin (not yet a canonical alien-language name).

The Margin is the living district where unwanted people make room for one another. Some were born there and have generations of neighborhood ties. Others are refugees from wars or collapsed economies, displaced port workers, people with expired permits, debtors, politically hunted dissidents, labor migrants, workers under contract disputes, stigmatized aliens, informal traders — and yes, smugglers, fugitives and predators who exploit the weak. There is no single 'outcast species' or binary moral class. The person selling soup can be someone whom official registries mark as 'irregular'; the person collecting protection fees may also finance a neighbor's clinic.

**Design tension:** the city needs their skills and labor but offers insufficient status or legal shelter. The Margin is **not outside the state**: some streets pay utility fees and taxes, the authorities issue citations, registered shops coexist with off-ledger labor. The legal system is selective and unpredictable, not absent.

## How did this neighborhood become part of the city?

Proposal: it began as practical support/service terraces by the old flood or utility drainage channel and the external road. When freight logistics expanded and residence permits grew more restrictive, temporary accommodation became permanent housing. Different generations added modular floors, repaired old infrastructure, built covered markets beneath supported elevated roads and learned to share expensive city services.

**Urban logic:** low rents are explained by older flood risk, noise from freight roads, poor official services, irregular property tenure and political stigma — not by universal collapse. The city administration knows the settlement exists, extracts value from its businesses, but finds comprehensive upgrading less politically attractive than selective policing.

The neighborhood connects to Old Junction via legitimate roads and dense informal foot/vehicle service links. A separate unreliable service corridor may skirt the drainage channel, but is **not** a magically safe smuggler motorway. Supported ring/viaduct columns are real: small repair businesses cluster beneath them where rents are low.

## Physical and visual identity: poor, maintained, full of life

From the taxi window:
- Mid-rise **compact retro-futurist homes**, alien-scale doorways and stoops, many generations of extensions and mismatched but purposeful repaired facade panels.
- Rented and improvised power submeters, communal charging stands, rooftop water condensers, cheap air filtration and reused analog-digital signage. Technologies may be old but **work**, because residents fix them.
- Muted colored paving, patched with different enamel/asphalt mixes. Drainage and curb barriers sometimes need attention, but a customer can still walk, shop, commute and sit outside.
- Warm practical lights, street-food steam, neighbors leaning from windows, repair benches, laundry or equivalent local personal objects, music and languages from overlapping storefronts.
- A well-run cooperative clinic, a cheap transit halt, a night market, a shared repair hall, households celebrating a festival. Not every facade is covered in threat graffiti.
- Marginal visual comfort: narrow plots, scarce private space, shared elevators and circulation, utility outages, noise, overcrowding and strained infrastructure. Do **not** erase poverty with fanciful pristine solarpunk luxury.

**Not** grimy random shipping-container stacks, medieval shacks with holograms, abandoned towers, collapsed highways, sewer-dwelling caricatures, perpetual purple neon or a road littered with bodies.

A powerful shot: a patched but beautiful street full of life under a large **physically supported** raised road, with cleaner Civic Core buildings on the distant skyline — an expensive city visibly passing above people it relies on.

## Three micro-areas — uneven danger, not a one-color crime zone

| Subarea (draft) | What's there | Why residents go there | What can go wrong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Lantern Row / residential heart** | Dense flats, food shops, shared service courts, neighborhood watch, clinic | home, children, community help and paid rides | rumor networks; landlord leverage; authority door checks |
| **Patchworks / workshop frontage** | Auto repair lanes, reused part stores, informal dispatch and taxi standing | repairs, tools, work, neighbors exchanging services | stolen-part provenance; protection fees; predatory credit |
| **The Cut / drainage edge** | Utility channel, cheap last-mile lodgings, closed-off service ramps, seasonal flow control | short routes, inexpensive shelter, delivery jobs | isolated extortion, unreliable lighting, hazards, sudden patrol sweeps |

These are everyday zones with gradients. At midday The Cut can be safe for a familiar resident; late at night a well-lit main road can be dangerous during an identification sweep. Poverty is not a direct deterministic danger rating.

## How this living neighborhood becomes playable without 3D interiors everywhere

**Confirmed:** The Margin is experienced from an actual **3D street/taxi** setting, while **most small enterprises, services and short social visits are 2D/VN location anchors**. The player recognizes a repair sign or storefront facade along an authored road; once they arrive at a legal/known stopping point, an illustrated counter, merchant portrait and Ink/service dialogue can present a rich experience without building a full 3D interior. These spots retain their place in the neighborhood and their prices, risks and owners across visits.

**Personal exceptions:** the player's **home and personal garage remain real 3D** spaces. A different vendor's workshop or a shop beside the garage usually needs only a 2D illustrated scene. Lantern Row food shops and local clinic, Patchworks used-parts vendors or gray dispatch desks, and a suspicious counter by The Cut can each support detailed 2D interactions. This is proposed content, not a new shipped shop system.

The most important social fact survives the transition: the vendor remembers the driver, debts and trust persist, and a transaction or mistaken phrase can create a story consequence or police exposure. None of these is a disconnected VN economy. See [Location Presentation Modes](LOCATION_PRESENTATION_MODES_V0.md).

## Who keeps the place functioning?

**Residents and shopkeepers** organize informal protection, utility repairs, care, food and local dispute resolution. Neighborhood networks can protect newcomers who contribute, while shutting out unknown outsiders. Residents retain **agency, dignity and internal political disagreement**.

**Small landlords and local permit brokers** can make informal residence feasible, but have leverage over tenants who cannot report abusive terms.

**Repair syndicates and gray dispatch operators** provide actual services, parts and fares that official platforms deny irregular drivers, charging for exposure risk.

**Protection operators** range from consensual paid neighborhood guards to extortionists; label their conduct by choices and accountability, not automatic 'gang == entire poor district'.

**Civic authorities** still enter. Inspections concentrate on transport hubs/permit doors or occur after political pressure and high-profile crime. Police absence during theft and over-presence during ID sweeps can both be true.

No faction owns all streets. Avoid total-safety 'friendly gang' or 'police never visit' shortcuts.

## Security/gameplay: dangerous for a specific human reason

For the illegally resident player, the Margin is relatively **less institutionally legible**: neighbors often use aliases, sublets and unregistered payments. That helps them blend into routines. It is **not truly safe**:
- Identity documentation scrutiny on their home block may be infrequent, but an angry landlord, a suspicious passenger or someone running extortion can expose them.
- Someone can provide cover, borrow the taxi, ask for a dangerous ride, or remember an inconsistent story. Help creates obligations, not immunity.
- Patrol sweeps, an expired electrical permit or a street crime can suddenly cause records and witness statements to be checked.
- Criminal predation exists, but it is **targeted, situational and visible**: route choice, time, obligations and introductions matter; no constant combat or pursuit gameplay.
- On a quiet night the player might safely buy food or help a neighbor. Emotional warmth and real danger should coexist, so the player has something worth protecting.

**Risk distinction:** in Civic Core, being noticed by an official sensor or license officer is the core fear. In the Margin, being *known by the wrong person* is the fear. Someone could even hate humans without knowing the driver is one; marginalized does not mean universally tolerant.

## The player's home and taxi garage (location decision)

**User-established direction:** the player **lives in the dangerous marginalized district**, not just at its border. Therefore a previous broad proposal to put home on the Old Junction/Outer Margin seam is superseded.

**Current suggested placement (not final building/scene data):**
- **Home:** a small, relatively tidy, properly inhabited rented room/flat **inside Lantern Row**, not a ruined bunker. Secondhand furniture, old communications console, practical personal items, modest privacy, utilities arranged through a landlord/shared building meter, nearby neighbors who may remember the player's habits.
- **Garage:** a narrow repair bay **within the Margin at the Patchworks side**, near a legal feeder road to Old Junction. The old taxi can reach work quickly without making the home itself a flashy taxi depot. Cheap tools, patched parts, access via local repair contacts, and some chance of an unannounced permit inspection.
- The distance between them can be a short local transition or authored small scene; **no new on-foot open-world requirement**.

The taxi is valuable enough to attract envy or questions; paid garage security is a tradeoff. The home should feel like the player's one real refuge, **but never an invulnerable UI safe zone**. Neither home nor garage positions currently change the live `defaultPersonalSpaceCatalog`, player save, or PR #74 navigation.

## Why it's financially poor but not physically destitute

Residents may have steady low-wage service jobs, seasonal freight shifts, unofficial remote work or pooled family support while still failing to qualify for recognized status, property rights, health coverage or official taxi apps. They repair rather than replace; rent rooms or share power contracts; lend each other credit and tools. **Poverty is an ongoing precarious budget and lack of bargaining power**, not a requirement to show wreckage on every corner.

The legal/gray economy is mutually dependent: the city buys off-ledger work and salvaged repair capacity while publicly disapproving of it. A neighborhood festival or a good food stall is not evidence that poverty is fictional; it shows social resilience.

## Designed sample taxi moments — not shipped content

1. An elderly resident asks for a cheap ride to the clinic, but the route through an official permit gate risks the driver's cover. Avoiding the gate costs fuel and time.
2. A garage neighbor offers a rare part sourced from a private estate. Works perfectly; provenance might later surface.
3. A dispatch broker has a lucrative pickup at The Cut. A rider offers extra cash to avoid police *and* asks the driver to be silent about the destination.
4. A local shopkeeper warns of a registration sweep, but asks for help delivering a family member first.
5. A seemingly harmless repeat passenger recognizes an unusual speech pattern; they need not be an informant, but the player must decide how to respond.

These are seeds for interactions and rumors, **not** acceptance of a street-combat, procedurally criminal city or new engine system.
